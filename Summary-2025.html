Background

Growing evidence indicates that most BC bat species overwinter within the province, with several remaining occasionally active through the winter. This activity has been documented acoustically in both cave and non-cave habitats (Lausen and Rae 2018), at cliff roosts suspected to be hibernacula in the Peace region (WLRS unpublished data), in coastal forests (WLRS unpublished data), and at five parks in southern BC (BC Parks unpublished data). Further year-round monitoring efforts likely exist beyond those noted here.

Although this activity spans diverse habitats (cliffs, old forests, and waterbodies) little is known about the specific non-cave microsites western bats use in winter (Nagorsen et al. 1994; Weller et al. 2018). Documented non-cave roosts include talus and tree roosts in southeast Alaska (Blejwas et al. 2021), tree cavities in southeastern BC (deFreitas 2023), a possible talus slope in the same region (Lausen and Hill 2021), and anthropogenic sites such as the siding and trim of houses on the coast (BC Community Bat Program unpublished data). Apart from several large hibernacula in southern BC mines, few hibernacula have been identified, suggesting that most of the region’s bats hibernate singly or in small, scattered groups. This mirrors findings from the western US, where wintering Myotis were found singly in 45% of records or in small groups with a median of two, and Townsend’s big-eared bats in slightly larger groups with a median of three (Weller et al. 2018) .

Understanding how bats use winter habitat has practical value. It can inform the management of recreational and industrial activities, and clarifying how bats use non-cave features such as rock crevices, talus, and trees may improve understanding of how Pseudogymnoascus destructans (the fungus that causes white-nose syndrome) spreads in BC, as well as potential treatment options.

Three studies in the Okanagan region, spanning the winter of 2011/12 to the present, form the basis of this report. The first, at Skaha Bluffs in 2011/12, revealed extensive winter activity, recording 1,551 bat passes over 105 sample nights (Sarell 2012) . At least eleven species were detected between 7 December 2011 and 31 March 2012 and are therefore thought to hibernate in the area.

BC Parks followed with a multi-year acoustic monitoring study across the Okanagan, running from 2019 through the winter of 2023 to locate potential hibernacula. It recorded ten species in late fall and early winter, and intermittently through the winter, all in rocky outcrops and canyons; this activity was initially interpreted as evidence of hibernacula near the detectors (Paterson 2024) . Field surveys in 2024/25, however, pointed to an alternative: bats may have been moving rapidly through these sites, using them as flight corridors rather than roosting nearby. Long-term detector data may therefore be a weaker indicator of local hibernation than first believed, and confirming whether bats actually roost in these features, and pinpointing the microsites they use, would substantially advance understanding of their winter ecology.

This motivated a shift in approach. In 2024/25, Skurikhina and Kellner (2025) established a capture site at Doctor’s Wall to locate hibernacula microhabitats directly. The pilot assessed the feasibility of fall and winter capture and tracking in South Okanagan parks and protected areas, and used radiotelemetry to identify the microsites tagged bats used. Specifically, it aimed to:

  • assess whether bats could be captured in late fall and early winter in the South Okanagan, particularly Skaha Park and Haynes Lease Ecological Reserve;

  • begin identifying the microsites bats use in late fall and winter; and

  • test the assumption underlying acoustic monitoring, that late fall and early winter detections indicate bats hibernating nearby, since relocating tagged bats by telemetry would confirm use of the immediate area.

In 2025/26 we also invested in data capture and sharing. All acoustic data from long-term monitoring sites (Paterson 2024; Skurikhina and Kellner 2025) were submitted to the North American Bat Monitoring Program database and to WildTrax, supporting larger-scale analyses and allowing the files to be stored and re-analysed in future. Data from Sarell (2012) were not added to these databases, but both of the other projects datasets were submitted to the BC Wildlife Species Inventory database.

Finally, we developed a data visualization tool that future results can be added to. Though simple at present, it is designed to be readily expanded into an interactive resource for examining patterns in winter bat detections across Okanagan parks and protected areas.

Methodology

Acoustic Monitoring

Passive acoustic monitoring was conducted using Wildlife Acoustic Song Meter MiniBat units, placed out for one to multiple nights, to confirm bat presence and aid in selecting suitable netting microsites. Occasional active monitoring was done during mist-netting, using a handheld Wildlife Acoustic Echometer Touch Pro to monitor bat activity at each capture site. Monitoring effort was not recorded for active monitoring (Table 1).  For the Sarell (2012) project acoustic monitoring was done with SM2 Bat and SM2Bat+ units from wildlife acoustics. Most sites were sampled as full-spectrum wave files but the Fortress, Wave, No Climbs and Great White site sin 2012 were sampled only with zero crossing.

Acoustic results for the sites sampled in 2012 were analyzed using Sonobat (version 3.05 Great Basin) if they were full spectrum wave files, and if they were zero-cross files thery were manually reviewd using Analook software. For all other projects data were analyzed using Kaleidoscope Pro (Wildlife Acoustics, version 5.6.8) with bat auto ID Bats of North America 5.8.1 modified for the region. The species list included Pallid Bats (Antrozous pallidus, ANPA), Townsend’s Big-eared Bat (Corynorhinus townsendii, COTO), Big Brown Bat (Eptesicus fuscus, EPFU), Hoary Bat (Lasiurus cinereus, LACI), Silver-haired Bat (Lasionycteris noctivagans, LANO), Californian Myotis (Myotis californicus, MYCA), Western small-footed Myotis (M. ciliolabrum, MYCI), Long-eared Myotis (M. evotis, MYEV), Little Brown Myotis (M. lucifugus, MYLU), Fringed Myotis (M. thysanodes, MYTH), Long-legged Myotis (M. volans, MYVO), and Yuma Myotis (M. yumanensis, MYYU). All files were manually reviewed by Brian Paterson and Mandy Kellner.

Summary of Results

We summarized results differently depending on the data available for each study. For Skurikhina and Kellner (2025) and Paterson (2024) , we worked from the raw detection data; for Sarell (2012) , we used the summary data reported in their own study. We then compiled all three datasets into a single dataframe recording the number of species identified per night at each site, along with site location and deployment and retrieval dates. Locations were kept separate even when they shared the same or similar names, so that distinct sites were not conflated in the summaries.

Data Submission

The long term acoustic monitoring data set for the Paterson (2024) and Skurikhina and Kellner (2025) studies has been formatted and submitted to BC Wildlife Species Inventory under project 6430 - South Okanagan Bat Hibernacula Project. Data has been also been submitted to NABat and can be viewed in the Partner Portal) under project: South Okanagan Bat Winter Bat Project (Project ID:16855). Analyzed acoustic files have been submitted to WildTrax and are available can be found under the project: South Okanagan Bat Winter Bat. Access to data sources will require permission from Mandy Kellner, and can be requested directly through the portals.

Results

Acoustic sampling happened in a total of 7 years, sampling a total of 20 unique sites Figure 1 . Across all survey years, Cougar Canyon had the highest number of recordings in a single year (2021), with a total of 13,428 detections. The greatest sampling effort in terms of site coverage occurred in 2011-12, during which 10 unique sites were monitored.

In total there have been 107978 bat recordings recorded over the length of the study. A total of 15 bat species were confirmed across all sites and years. The most frequently recorded species was LANO, with 20,051 detections.

Discussion

There are several underground cave/talus-cave systems known to BC Parks. Assessment of bat use, species presence, and timing of use could be accomplished through acoustic monitoring and/or guano collection. One such area is at Doctor’s wall; during this survey, nets were set up near the entrances of identified caves, but no bats were detected during emergence or caught in the nets. One of the project partners set up an experimental acoustic detector inside the larger cave on November 12, 2024. As of March 2025, this detector had not been retrieved. Options for future work include additional placement of bat detectors at openings or inside caves, single-species DNA analysis through collection of single pellets of guano in paper envelopes (Wildlife Genetics International), and/or multi-species DNA analysis through collection of multiple guano samples (Northern Arizona University Species from Feces lab).

Recommendations

  1. Explore use of detector array to increase understanding of bat winter movements and potential travel corridors as well as inform capture efforts.
  2. Target Gilles Creek and the Spring for additional monitoring and capture work as the area currently believed to offer the best chance of successful captures.
  3. Increase understanding of bat use of cave/talus features that are known or suspected to be used by bats, through passive acoustic monitoring and guano collection to determine timing of use and species.
  4. Expand collection of existing data. This project aims to identify and include additional acoustic datasets. Possible sources including data from 2025/26 Skaha Park winter monitoring (Kellner pers comm), and ECCC/CWS year-round monitoring at Vaseaux (T. Luszcz pers comm)
